When a child won't eat at the canteen

It's the call we get most often in September. The child comes home saying they haven't eaten, the parents get anxious, and a cycle starts that usually makes things worse. Here's how we see it from inside the canteen.

Why it happens

There are very different reasons behind an "I don't want to eat", and mixing them up is the first mistake:

  • It's unfamiliar food. A child used to a short list of dishes is faced with plates they don't recognise. That's not rejection, it's unfamiliarity.
  • There's noise and rushing. A packed canteen with exactly forty minutes doesn't invite anyone to try something new.
  • It's about independence. Around three or four, deciding what goes in their mouth is one of the few things a child actually controls.
  • They're tired or still adjusting. In the first weeks of term, many children simply eat less from sheer tiredness.
  • Some textures are hard for them. Some children are genuinely sensitive to certain textures, and that isn't fussiness.

What doesn't work

  • Forcing a plate to be finished. It builds resistance and disconnects a child from their own hunger and fullness signals.
  • Bargaining with dessert. It turns vegetables into a toll and dessert into a prize. It reinforces exactly the opposite of what you want.
  • Comparing with others. "Your brother eats everything" has never worked on anyone.
  • Talking about it constantly at home. If every evening turns into an interrogation about what they ate, the whole subject grows until it takes over.
  • Hiding vegetables. Blending them into a sauce gets them eaten today, but the child never learns to recognise them.

What actually helps

  • Offering without pressure, again and again. A new food can take ten or fifteen exposures before it's accepted. Counting them is our job; the patience needed on your side is the hard part.
  • Letting them serve themselves. Deciding the amount completely changes their willingness to try something.
  • An adult sitting down, eating the same food. It's the single most effective thing, and the one that happens least.
  • Time. If there's a rush to clear the table, there's no room to explore.
  • Talking about food outside mealtimes. Cooking together, going to the market, growing a pot of basil. All of that moves things more than any conversation at the table.

What you can say at home

Ask about the experience, not the plate. "Who did you sit with?", "what did you talk about?", "was there anything you liked?" Once the conversation stops circling the amount eaten, the tension usually drops, and appetite often comes back on its own.

And in the evening, serve a normal dinner. Making up for it with a favourite dish because "they didn't eat at lunch" teaches a child that skipping lunch pays off.

When it's worth getting advice

Eating little or being selective is common and usually sorts itself out with time. That said, if there's weight loss, the range of foods they'll eat keeps shrinking instead of growing, mealtimes bring real pain or distress, or you're genuinely worried, talk to your paediatrician. We can support what happens at the table, but a diagnosis isn't something we're in a position to make.

What the canteen should be doing

If your child is eating badly, the school should be able to explain how they're supporting your child, not just report that they didn't eat. Ask whether an adult sits at the table, whether children can serve themselves, how much time they're given, and how progress gets tracked over the weeks.
